The Weight of Stones

A short story

The old woman at the trailhead didn't ask where he was going. She asked how long he'd been carrying it.

Marcus thought she meant the pack. Forty liters, which he'd weighed at the airport in Atlanta and again at the bus station in Tanabe. Eleven point four kilos both times. He'd been precise about it. Precision was something he could do when everything else had gone sideways.

"The pack," he said. "Since yesterday."

She shook her head. She was sitting on a wooden bench outside the pilgrim center, peeling a mandarin orange with fingers that were mostly knuckle. Behind her, a poster showed the route in cross-section: the mountains like a cardiogram, each peak labeled with a name he couldn't read.

"Not the pack," she said in English that was better than his Japanese. She dropped a curl of peel into her lap and looked at him the way a doctor looks at an X-ray, not at the patient but at the thing inside the patient that doesn't belong.

He adjusted the hip belt and walked past her into the cedars.

The trail climbed for the first two hours without apology. Stone steps set into the mountain centuries ago, each one worn into a shallow bowl by feet he'd never know. Moss covered everything the rain could reach, which was everything. The air was so wet it had texture. He breathed it like broth.

He had read about this. The Nakahechi route, the imperial pilgrimage road, the one the emperors walked when they wanted to feel close to something they couldn't tax or command. He'd read about the torii gates and the shrines and the hot springs. He'd read about the proper way to bow, the proper way to wash, the proper number of claps. He had prepared the way he prepared for everything, which was thoroughly, and which had never once protected him from the thing he was preparing for.

Claire had died in April. Five months ago. Five months that felt like a new unit of time, something between a week and a decade, too short to have survived and too long to still feel this liquefied. She had been forty-one. They had been married for nine years. She had been a public defender with a smoker's laugh despite never having smoked, and she'd had a way of reading in bed that made the whole mattress vibrate slightly because she laughed at things silently, with her stomach, the way people laugh when they've forgotten anyone is watching.

He had not come to Japan to grieve. He had come to Japan because he had bought the tickets in February, when Claire was in remission and they were celebrating by planning things. Ambitious things. Things that assumed a future. The oncologist had used the word "durable" and they had both latched onto it the way you latch onto a word when you need it to be a prophecy.

The tickets were nonrefundable. Claire would have found that funny.

By noon he reached Takahara, the village in the mist. It earned its name. Clouds sat in the valley below like cotton stuffed into a drawer, and the village floated above them — a few wooden houses, a shrine, a vending machine selling both hot coffee and cold beer with equal indifference to the hour.

He bought the coffee. He sat on the steps of the shrine and drank it and thought about nothing successfully for almost forty seconds.

Then he thought about the bedroom. The last week. The morphine and the way it made her generous in a way that frightened him, as though she were already giving things away. She'd told him to come to Japan anyway. She'd told him to walk the trail. She'd said, "You're going to want to carry me with you, and I need you to not do that." She'd said it clearly, without metaphor, the way she talked to juries.

He had promised.

He was breaking the promise with every step. He could feel her in the weight of the pack, in the ache of his knees, in the specific way the light filtered through cedar canopy and reminded him of the afternoon they'd driven through Olympic National Forest and she'd made him pull over because the light was "doing something." The light was always doing something. He just hadn't learned to notice until she'd taught him, and now he couldn't stop.

The trail descended through bamboo into a valley where the river ran the color of jade. He crossed a suspension bridge that swayed like a drunk's confidence and climbed again. His knees announced themselves. His shoulders filed complaints. The pack had gained weight, or he had lost strength, or both.

At three o'clock he reached the guesthouse in Chikatsuyu. A woman showed him to a room with a futon on tatami and a window that opened onto a garden the size of a parking space but arranged with such care that it felt infinite. There was a stone basin. There was a maple tree. There was a moss-covered rock shaped like a sleeping cat, and he was almost certain this was intentional.

He showered in the communal bath and sat in water so hot it simplified his nervous system to a single signal. For fifteen minutes he was only a body. It was the longest he'd gone without thinking since April.

At dinner he sat at a low table with three other pilgrims. A German couple in their sixties who held hands under the table like teenagers. A Japanese man in his thirties who was walking the trail for the fourth time and spoke about it the way some people speak about a relationship that keeps teaching them things.

"Each time I walk, the trail is the same," the man said. "I am different. The difference is the lesson."

Marcus wanted to find this insufferable. He almost did. But the man said it without performance, the way you say something you've tested against your own life and found to be true.

"What did you learn this time?" the German woman asked.

The man considered this. He poured tea for everyone before answering, which Marcus would later understand was itself part of the answer.

"That the weight you notice is never the real weight," he said.

He didn't sleep. The futon was fine. The room was silent except for frogs doing whatever frogs do at two in the morning, which apparently was everything. He lay on his back and watched the ceiling and had the conversation with Claire that he'd been rehearsing for five months.

He told her about the trail. He told her about the stone steps shaped like bowls and the light in the cedars and the suspension bridge. He told her about the coffee at the shrine and the cat-shaped rock. He told her these things in the second person, as though she were listening, because the alternative was the third person, which would make her a character in a story instead of the person he was talking to.

He told her he was trying not to carry her.

He told her he was failing.

He told her about the man at dinner, the one who'd said the weight you notice is never the real weight, and he waited for her to say something back, the way she would have, something sharp and funny and slightly deflating, something that would make the profundity bearable by cutting it down to size.

She didn't say anything. She hadn't said anything in five months. That was the whole problem, stated plainly: the person who made everything bearable was the thing he had to bear.

He walked for two more days. The trail broke him open in stages. It rained and the stone steps became streams and he slipped twice and caught himself once and didn't catch himself the second time and sat in the mud for a while considering whether this was a metaphor. It wasn't. It was mud. Claire would have said that. She would have said, "Marcus, it's mud. Get up."

He got up.

On the third morning, approaching Kumano Hongu Taisha, the great shrine, he came around a bend in the trail and saw the torii gate. It was enormous and black and it stood at the top of a stone staircase that rose out of the forest like a sentence that had been building for miles. The trees fell away. The sky opened. The gate framed nothing but sky and the implication that what lay beyond it was different from what came before.

He stopped at the bottom of the stairs. There were other pilgrims — a school group, a few solo walkers, an elderly couple taking photographs of each other with the seriousness of people documenting evidence. He watched them pass through the gate and become briefly silhouetted against the sky and then descend into the shrine grounds on the other side.

He took off his pack. He set it on the ground beside the stairs. He stood there for a long time, feeling the absence of its weight, which was worse than its presence because at least the weight was something to push against, something to resent, something to carry.

The old woman from the trailhead was not there. She was two days and forty kilometers behind him. But he heard her question clearly, the way you hear things that were never really questions: How long have you been carrying it?

He picked up the pack. It weighed eleven point four kilos. It had weighed eleven point four kilos at the airport in Atlanta and at the bus station in Tanabe and at every point along the trail. It had not changed. He had not changed either, not in any way he could measure, not in any way that would satisfy a scale or a spreadsheet or the part of him that believed precision could protect him.

But he was at the bottom of the stairs, and the gate was at the top, and Claire had told him to walk. Not to carry. Not to arrive. To walk.

He walked.
